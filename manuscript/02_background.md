# Background

Suspected neonatal infection is among the commonest reasons for admission to newborn units in low- and middle-income countries, and neonatal sepsis remains a leading cause of neonatal death [CITE: global and sub-Saharan African burden of neonatal sepsis and possible serious bacterial infection; Kenya-specific neonatal mortality data]. Its early signs are non-specific, so clinicians must decide quickly, often on incomplete information, how to assess the newborn, which investigations to request, how severe the illness is, and which antibiotics to give and for how long [CITE: clinical presentation and diagnostic uncertainty of neonatal sepsis]. Inappropriate or prolonged empirical antibiotic use contributes to antimicrobial resistance, which is already high among neonatal pathogens in the region [CITE: antimicrobial resistance in neonatal sepsis isolates, sub-Saharan Africa / Kenya].

International and national guidance covers each of these steps. Examples include the World Health Organization recommendations for managing serious bacterial infection in infants aged 0–59 days, Kenya's Comprehensive Newborn Care Protocols and National Antibiotic Use Guidelines, and the NICE guideline on neonatal infection [CITE: the four guideline documents, as listed in Methods]. Adherence to such guidance in routine newborn care is variable [CITE: audits of guideline adherence in Kenyan or LMIC newborn units]. The guidance is long, spread across documents, and not always easy to consult at the point of care [CITE: barriers to guideline use at the point of care in LMIC hospitals].

Large language models (LLMs) can answer clinical questions in fluent natural language, and several have performed well on medical examination questions and clinical vignettes [CITE: LLM performance on medical licensing examinations and vignette benchmarks]. They can also produce plausible but unsupported or incorrect statements, and their answers may not reflect local guidance or resource constraints [CITE: hallucination and safety failures of LLMs in clinical question answering]. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) addresses part of this problem. Answers are generated from passages retrieved from a defined document collection, and each statement can be traced to its source [CITE: RAG for clinical question answering; grounding and citation in medical RAG]. Whether such an answer is useful depends on two things: whether the right guideline passages are retrieved, and whether clinicians judge the resulting answer to be accurate, safe and appropriate to their setting.

Most evaluations of medical LLMs use examination questions or clinical vignettes written for the study, usually from high-income settings [CITE: reviews of medical LLM evaluation methods and settings]. A recent Kenyan study benchmarked general-purpose LLMs against clinicians on locally generated primary-care vignettes, using blinded physician ratings on an 11-domain rubric [REF: Mwaniki P, Musau W, Isaaka L, et al. Benchmarking large language models and clinicians using locally generated primary healthcare vignettes in Kenya. BMJ Digital Health & AI 2026. doi:10.1136/bmjdh-2026-000081 — verified via Europe PMC PMC13528382; check page/article details at proofing]. Less is known about RAG pipelines restricted to local guidelines and queried with questions generated from routinely collected patient records. In particular, it is unclear how the way a patient record is turned into a retrieval query affects which guideline passages are retrieved [CITE: query construction or query expansion from structured EHR data for clinical retrieval]. A related question concerns the risk categories that could accompany such answers. Newborns with signs of possible serious bacterial infection in Kenya have been grouped into five mortality-risk categories by a latent class model, developed and externally validated in routine data [REF: Tuti et al. 2026]. Statistical validation does not show whether clinicians find a category credible for an individual newborn, which matters if the categories are to be used in care [CITE: clinician acceptability of risk-stratification or prediction tools].

We therefore conducted an exploratory, offline evaluation of a RAG pipeline that answers questions about suspected neonatal infection using only local clinical guidelines. The questions were generated from routinely collected, de-identified newborn-unit records and covered four aspects of care: assessment, investigations, severity classification and antibiotic course. The intended users are clinicians in newborn units; the pipeline reports and cites what the guidelines say and does not give independent clinical advice. Our primary objective was to describe clinician ratings of the pipeline's guideline-grounded answers on the 11 domains of the PATH LLM evaluation rubric [REF: PATH LLM Playbook, https://media.path.org/documents/LLM_Playbook_final.pdf — full citation details TODO], overall and by query area. Secondary objectives were to estimate inter-rater agreement, to describe whether clinicians would accept the answers, to describe how far clinicians agree with the triage category assigned to each record by the latent class model, and to explore how retrieval of the cited guideline passages depends on the way the query is constructed from the record. No hypothesis was prespecified.
